What this is

This is the Tipiṭaka — พระไตรปิฎก, the three baskets — and the whole scholastic and homiletic apparatus a northern monastery needed to teach, preach and perform it. At 2,350 witnesses it is the largest genre in the collection, a third of all 6,990 catalogued manuscripts. The catalogue's own raw labels split it five ways: Sutta 973 (the discourses), Vinaya 494 (monastic discipline), Abhidhamma 273 (the systematic analysis), a broad General Buddhism remainder of 363, and Anisong — 234 ānisaṃsa sermons on the merit that accrues from one specific pious act.

It is easy to file all this under "background" and move on. Don't. The canon in this archive is not an abstraction: it is a physical event that happened at particular temples in particular years, and most of it can be dated to a single campaign with a single peak year.

The year they copied the canon

Sort every dated manuscript from Wat Sung Men in Phrae by year and one figure stands out so far it looks like an error: 404 manuscripts carry the date CS 1198 — 1836 CE. Not 404 over a decade. In one year. Of those, 233 are canonical: 89 Sutta, 68 Abhidhamma, 60 Vinaya, plus the philology and chronicle volumes that go with them. The surrounding years are not quiet either — 80 in 1833, 87 in 1834, 84 in 1837, and a second surge of 144 in 1842. Across the whole genre, the 1830s alone account for 484 dated canonical witnesses at this one temple, with 151 more in the 1840s.

Inference — that is not the ordinary attrition-and-replacement copying of a working library. That is a campaign: scribes recruited, palm prepared, texts divided into fascicles and parcelled out, an entire Tipiṭaka pushed through a scriptorium inside about fifteen years, with 1836 as its hinge. The evidence is purely in the dates and the volume, but the dates are unambiguous, and 307 of the 1836 Sung Men manuscripts are recorded as carrying a colophon — the copyists signed their work.

Tradition holds — Wat Sung Men remembers itself as exactly this: a temple whose scripture-hall drew rulers from across Lanna and Lan Chang to venerate the Tipiṭaka kept there, and whose great abbot of the early nineteenth century organised the gathering and copying of the canon on a scale nobody in the region had attempted. The archive cannot confirm a name from its metadata. What the archive can confirm is the shadow the project cast: 1,014 of these 2,350 canonical witnesses — 43% of the entire genre — come from this single temple, and 1,780 manuscripts of all kinds. Read Phrae's apparent 45% provincial dominance as the survival of one extraordinary library, not as a map of where the canon was used.

A fourth basket

The most distinctively Lanna object in the whole genre is a bundle whose title is just a list. 77 manuscripts carry a title naming the piṭaka as a set — and 48 of those 77 name a fourth member: Winai pitaka, Suttanta pitaka, Aphithamma pitaka, Anisong pitaka. วินัยปิฏกะ, สุตตันตะปิฏกะ, อภิธัมมา, อานิสงปิฏกะ. The merit-sermons, promoted to stand beside the three canonical baskets as if they were one.

Sometimes the fascicle numbers are written into the title, and the object gives itself away entirely: ms 635 at Wat Na Pang in Nan (1790) reads Suttanta piṭaka fascicle one, Vinaya piṭaka fascicle two, Abhidhamma piṭaka fascicle three, Anisong piṭaka — a single bundle holding one short reading from each basket in order, and then the sermon that says what merit the donor has just earned. Inference — this is the canon in miniature, made to be preached at a dedication: you sponsor a copy of the scriptures, the monks read a taste of each basket aloud, and then they read you your receipt. The sequence is a liturgy, not a shelf.

These bundles run 1669 to 1930 and cluster at Wat Sung Men (37) and Wat Ban Luk in Lampang (16). The cataloguer filed them under whichever basket happened to come first in the title, which is why the same object appears in this archive as Vinaya (33 of them), Sutta (18), Abhidhamma (7) and Anisong (6). Inference — one of the cleanest taxonomy repairs available here is to pull the piṭaka-set bundle out as its own form. Browse the genre and you can see the duplicates scattered across four headings.

The merit of the thing you are holding

The Anisong shelf — 234 witnesses, browsable as its own sub-genre — is where the canon touches ordinary life, and it is the least abstract writing in this archive. Each text answers one question: what do I get for doing this exact thing? And the exact things are wonderfully, specifically small.

  • อานิสง เข้าซาวเมด — the merit of twenty grains of rice. The oldest dated Anisong witness in the collection, 1638, with a second copy at Wat Phra That Chang Kham in Nan dated 1880 and a variant title, อานิสงอันหื้เข้าชาวเม็ดเปันทาน, at Wat Kittiwong in Mae Hong Son in 1717.
  • อานิสงหมากคำ — the merit of one mouthful of betel (Wat Phothanaram, Chiang Rai, 1931).
  • อานิสงกองหยากเหืยอ — the merit of a rubbish heap (Wat Klang, Phrae).
  • อานิสงบอกไฟ — the merit of the rocket, the bamboo skyrocket fired to bring rain; three witnesses, one of them bundled with the merit of a vihāra, a prasat and a scripture-chest in a single 1808 fascicle at Wat Na Pang, Nan.
  • อานิสงหนูเผือกตัวเดียว — the merit of a single white mouse, copied at Wat Sung Men in 1880 onto sixteen folios. Six witnesses of the white-mouse story survive across the whole archive, one of them retitled the tale of King White Mouse.
  • อานิสงเลี้ยงพ่อแม่ — the merit of looking after your parents, which turns up bound together with the merit of building a bridge, a well, a rest-pavilion and a bathing-place, all in one 1933 fascicle at Wat Si Khom Kham in Phayao.

And then the loop closes. Eleven witnesses preach the merit of writing or making the Dhamma itself — อานิสงอันได้ส้างเขียนธัมม, Wat Pa Phlu, Lamphun, 1848 — and seven more preach the merit of building the chest or the hall to keep it in: อานิสงส้างหีดธัมม์, the scripture-chest, and อานิสงส้างหอธัมม, the ho trai. Wat Sung Men even copied out the merit of the piṭaka itself in 1834, mid-campaign. Inference — somewhere in the 1830s a Sung Men donor sat and listened to a monk read out the merit of paying scribes to copy palm-leaf scriptures, and the sermon he was listening to was itself one of the palm-leaf scriptures the campaign was copying. The collection contains the argument that created it.

Why "canonical" understates it

Very little of this is bare transmitted scripture. Two Lan Na forms dominate, and both are local intellectual labour:

  • นิสัย / nissaya — the Pali carried line by line with an interlinear gloss in Lan Na vernacular. 42 witnesses in the Sutta basket alone, and the characteristic shape of northern monastic teaching. It is why the canon and the grammar & lexicography corpus overlap so heavily: a nissaya is a construe, and a construe is philology.
  • โวหาร / wohan — the vernacular preaching version. 211 of the 973 Sutta witnesses, more than a fifth, carry Wohan in the title, against 22 Vinaya, 15 Abhidhamma and 41 in the General Buddhism remainder. Inference — the north copied the discourses overwhelmingly in order to say them out loud to laypeople, not to shelve them.

A third form is pure lexicon: ศัพท์ / นามศัพท์ word-glossaries, which take 91 of the 494 Vinaya witnesses. To file all of this simply as "canonical" undercounts, badly, how much of this genre is northern scholarship about the canon rather than the canon itself.

Where the canon leaks

Exactly three of the 2,350 are flagged research-priority by the project's own esoteric-subject screen, and all three are the same kind of seam:

  • Suat mon thon tang tang — assorted thon chants, Wat Chedi Luang, Chiang Mai, 1865.
  • Rathana thao thang si — the invitation of the Four Great Kings, Wat Ban Ueam, Lampang.
  • Kammawaca, Sut thon pha lae bat — the formal act of the saṅgha bound in one hand with a thon rite for a robe and a bowl, at Wat Si Khom Kham, Phayao.

That last one is the whole argument of this archive in a single object. And it is not alone: ms 4954 at Wat Phra That Lampang Luang is catalogued as Vinaya and its contents are monastic ritual procedure, astrology, and chants — พิธีกรรมสงฆ์, โหราศาสตร์, บทสวดมนต์ — sharing one bundle without apology.

The clearest case is a single text. อุณหัสสวิชัย, Uṇhissavijaya — tradition holds it is the verse a deity brings down to a man whose death has been announced, and reciting it buys him the years back — appears twelve times in this collection and is filed under three different genres: once as Sutta, seven times as Anisong, four times as Chanting. It is the same text each time. Which basket it belongs to depends entirely on what the cataloguer thought it was for. Read it beside the life-extension rite, where the same logic runs without any canonical cover at all.

The shape of the collection

Overwhelmingly Tham Lanna (2,000 of 2,350, 85%) on palm-leaf (2,192, 93%) — the canonical support in its purest form, a bound stack copied for permanence. Mulberry paper takes only 142. Language runs 1,567 Pali-and-Lan Na against 611 monolingual Pali, with a real Tai fringe: 85 Pali-and-Lao, 46 Pali-and-Shan, 23 Pali-Shan-Burmese, plus 261 witnesses in Tham Lao script, 71 in Shan and 11 in Burmese. Dated witnesses number 1,739 and run 1495 to 1985, peaking hard in the 19th century (1,127 of them). Provinces: Phrae 1,064, Lampang 424, Chiang Mai 218, Nan 206, Phayao 124, Mae Hong Son 101. 2,348 of the 2,350 carry a IIIF manifest — very nearly all of this is already viewable page by page.

The oldest dated witness in the whole genre — and in the whole archive — is the Milindapañha, the debate between the Greek king Menander and the elder Nāgasena, copied in monolingual Pali at Wat Lai Hin Luang in Lampang in CS 857, 1495 CE. Two years later the same library dated a Pali Yamaka. Inference — before Sung Men's campaign there was already a working Pali scholastic library in Ko Kha, and its two earliest survivors are a philosophical dialogue and the hardest book in the Abhidhamma. Wherever the north's reputation for scholastic seriousness comes from, it did not start in the 1830s.

Notes

  • One collection drives the whole distribution. Wat Sung Men's 1,014 witnesses are 43% of the genre. Treat the province and temple counts as a map of where a great library was preserved, not where canon was produced or used — every muang recited the suttas; one library kept the copies. It has an article of its own, a place page, and a pin on the Atlas.
  • The taxonomy's coarsest bucket. Transmitted canon, nissaya construes, wohan preaching versions, ศัพท์ glossaries, the para-canonical anisong and the piṭaka-set bundle all sit under one heading. Splitting them is the highest-value pass available on this genre, and would move a large share toward grammar and liturgy.
  • Read alongside the birth-stories. The Khuddaka-nikāya jātaka were pulled into their own Jātaka genre — 1,633 witnesses — so this heading is the canon minus its most-copied narrative layer. The Vessantara of the Mahāchat is over there, and the merit-sermon that goes with it (Anisong mahawetsantala cadok, Wat Si Khom Kham, 1860) is over here. One corpus, split by a taxonomy.
  • A crawl priority worth naming. The bundles are where the surprises are. Any untitled canonical fascicle from Nan, Phayao or Mae Hong Son that a title search reports as one text may hold four, and the fourth may be a katha or a horoscope.
