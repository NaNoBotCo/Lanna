Lersi / Ruesi (ascetic-seers)

No representative image is catalogued for this subject yet.

What this is

A lersi (ฤๅษี, also ruesi, reusi) is the ascetic-seer — the Thai form of the Sanskrit ṛṣi, the forest hermit who mastered the arts and then gave them away. He is not one figure but a class of teacher-ancestors: in the tradition's own telling, the originators and holders of wichaa itself — astrology, alchemy, herbal medicine, the drawing of yantra, the reciting of katha. Nothing in this archive begins with itself. Every art in it claims a seer at the far end of its line, and the way that claim gets made is not a footnote but a rite: before you write a syllable, mould a figure, boil a salve or cast a horoscope, you say the seers' names out loud.

This page follows that rite through the corpus. It turns out to be one of the best-attested things in the whole collection — and, at the same time, one of the least visible, because it lives in the first two pages of books whose titles are about something else. The profile box beside this article says 3 manuscripts, and those three are a joke the tradition is playing on the index: all three are modern meditation volumes that carry a seer's name only as a famous abbot's nickname. The real attestation runs through 25 of the 31 contributed volumes, and not one line of it is in a title.

The seer as the tradition draws him

Tradition holds — the picture is fixed and instantly legible: an old man with a long beard and moustache, hair bound into a topknot under a tall pointed cap or a jewelled headband, a rosary at the neck, and — the giveaway — a tiger- or leopard-striped cloth wrapped around the hips. He lives in a cave or a forest tract. He is not a monk; he keeps no Vinaya, ordains nobody, and answers to no abbot. He is what Buddhist Southeast Asia does with the older Indian sage: too powerful to disown, too irregular to absorb, so he is seated one rank below the Buddha and one rank above everybody who wants his knowledge.

You can check the costume against the corpus without leaving this page. The Ancient Thai Massage Manual prints hermit after hermit across its back half, and the description repeats almost word for word from figure to figure — tall pointed headdress, long beard and moustache, rosary at the neck, tiger-striped lower cloth (p.83, p.85, p.87, p.89). Whoever drew them was working from an iconographic rule, not from life.

Saying the hundred and eight names

The most-repeated lersi passage in the corpus is not a story about a seer. It is the wai khru — the homage to teachers — and it is the thing a practitioner does before doing anything at all.

The Compendium of Ancient Katha opens with it (p.2–3). It starts in impeccable Pali: the Namo three times, the three refuges, the Itipi so, the invitation to the devas of mountain peaks and palaces and thickets and paddy fields. Then the language drops into Thai and the list turns strange and specific. The reciter invites the virtue of his father and mother, of his preceptor and teachers, and then, by name: พระฤๅษีนารอท (Narot), นาไลย (Nalai), กระไลยโกฏิ (Krailaikoti), ตาวัว — Cow-Eye — ตาไฟ — Fire-Eye — กัสสะปะ (Kassapa), ไกรภพ (Kraiphop), ทัสนะมงคล (Tassanamongkhon), and เพชรฉลูกัญ (Phetchalu), "together with the accomplished masters of knowledge." After them come the Earth Goddess, Mother Ganga, the Fire God, the Wind God and Īśvara. And then the sentence the whole tradition rests on: พระฤๅษีทั้งร้อยแปดตน — "and the hermit-sages, all one hundred and eight."

The conventional 108 is not folklore about this material. It is printed inside it.

Turn the page and the invitation becomes a description of where each power is to sit in your body while you work: the Buddha above the crown of the head, Brahmā on the left shoulder, Nārāyaṇa on the right, Mother Ganga as your saliva, the Wind God as the breath of your mouth, the nāga-king as your necklace, and Phra Kan — the dark lord — as your heart (p.3). Only then: "may the teachers dwelling in caves come and bestow blessing upon me." Inference — the rite is not petition so much as assembly. You are being rebuilt, part by part, out of borrowed authorities, and the seers are the part that supplies the knowing.

Four separately printed volumes in this archive carry substantially the same roster, which is how you can tell it is a fixed text rather than one author's invention:

  • the Compendium of Ancient Katha (p.2–3);
  • the Vedic Katha for Chai Chatri, where the seers appear three times over — in a long "virtue of…" litany that runs past Vessuvaṇa, Yama, the Four Guardian Kings and King Phali before it reaches ฤๅษีตาวัว and ฤๅษีตาไฟ (p.4–5); in a compact Verse for Gathering the Teachers (p.6); and again in the body-station passage at p.8;
  • the Yantra Inscription Formulas in Mixed Khom and Thai (p.30), roster identical, spellings shifted;
  • the Life of Luang Por Thob (p.51), which prints a shortened form — Narot, Nalai, Ta Wua, Ta Fai, "and all 108 sage-hermits" — and adds an instruction that reads like a stage direction: may they "come and rest upon my head."

Inference — the variants are informative. Where three books read ไกรภพ Kraiphop, the Vedic Katha volume reads ไตรภพ Traiphop (p.6) — and ไตรภพ is also the name signed under two of the hermit-exercise verses in the massage manual (p.87, p.89). Whether that is one figure, a pen-name, or a copyist's slip is exactly the kind of question the corpus can now be asked, and could not be before.

Moulding a seer out of termite earth

The Treatise on Making Sacred Charms and Amulets advertises on its own contents page that it holds 42 kinds of amulet-manual and 158 ways of making things (p.15). Item three on that list, filed between moulding a deva figure and boiling love-charm oil, is moulding a figure of the Lersi — and the book gives not one recipe for it but three (p.34–35).

The first is for the person who wants to become a healer and to hold mantras "so they rise." Take earth from the tops of termite mounds, gathered from seven separate places, and mould the Great Lersi out of it. Then take seven leaves — chumsaeng, ratchaphruek, makham-kai, matum, khan-song, kanlapaphruek, chaiyaphruek — and insert all seven into the figure's body. Lay out kraya-buat, the ascetic's offering-food, worship properly, and chant the lersi-katha to 108, then the Itipiso to 108. After that, the treatise says, whenever you pound a medicine or begin any work at all, ask that figure for its efficacy. And then it closes the recipe with a line that is the whole theology of the practice in one sentence: it is said that if anyone comes upon this treatise, it is as though he had met the Lersi in person.

The third formula is more exact and more macabre. Termite-mound earth from seven places again — but now mixed with earth taken from the middle of a charnel-ground, also from seven places. Mould him seated cross-legged in meditation, left hand in the lap, right hand holding a rosary. Then inscribe the numerals into the body: 1 at the peak of the crown, 9 on both upper arms, 2 on both knees, 7 once on the base in front. Consecrate the seven leaves 108 times, insert them, wake the figure a second time with bai-si and ascetic food standing by. Thereafter, when you want to work: "invite the Lersi to come and be seated, wake him with that katha three or seven times, speak well, and ask for the blessing of efficacy."

Inference — note what the numerals do. The figure is not decorated; it is addressed — crown, arms, knees, front — the same body-station logic the wai khru uses when it seats Brahmā on your shoulder and the Wind God in your mouth. The seer's image and the practitioner's body are built by the same method. And the material is the tradition's own argument: termite-mound earth is soil raised out of the ground by something living and unseen, and the charnel-ground is where the dead go on being useful. The seer is moulded from the two places where the world is thinnest.

The seer inside the body — ruesi dat ton

The strangest and richest lersi holding in the archive is a medical book. The Ancient Thai Massage Manual opens under the name of Doctor Jīvaka Komārabhacca and then, from p.78 to p.191, runs a continuous plate-book of ruesi dat ton (ฤๅษีดัดตน) — "the hermit twisting his own body." 68 of its pages carry a ดัดตนแก้… heading — a stretch to cure such-and-such — and 77 of its pages name the ฤๅษี outright. Each plate is the same three things: a hermit in the standard costume holding one posture, a short Thai verse in khlong metre, and a signature.

Read three of them and the form becomes clear.

  • To cure stomach ache and the ankle joints (p.87): kneel upright, chest lifted, reach one arm overhead and behind to catch the foot folded up behind the head. The verse names its author — not the illustrator, the originator: วิศวามิตรเผาดง, "Viśvāmitra, who burned the forest, taught this method." Signed ไตรภพ.
  • To cure wind in the foot (p.89): the ascetic Svāmitra "held his body upright, walking through forest, wilderness and thicket" — and the verse then explains the ailment as an occupational injury of that life: "the foot pain that arises comes from prolonged weariness." Raise a hand to cover the face; sit in meditation posture.
  • To cure wind in the throat and the wind of cramp (p.101), which takes two hermits: "the two companions having knelt, turning their faces to look aside, Vasiṭṭha braces and treads firm upon the joint, the vital point shifting, while the might of Chandamat swirls and curls the numbed limb." This is a massage, and the practitioner's hands have been given a rishi's name.

The signatures are the other half of the story. They are not hermits at all: Phraratchaniphon — "a royal composition" (p.83); Phra Ong Chao Siring (p.85); Phra Ariyawongsamuni (p.93), whose verse pauses mid-instruction to remember that its sage "drew out the heart of the ten-necked one" — Rāvaṇa; Phra Rattana Muni (p.97), Phraya Ratchamontri Borirak (p.99), Phra Yan Pariyat (p.101), Krom Muen Uditanuphap (p.81), Phra Maha Chuang Parian (p.133), Phra Ong Chao Khanechon (p.177). Tradition holds — this is the Bangkok-court hermit-exercise corpus, versified by princes, nobles and senior monks and set beside statues in a temple courtyard so that anyone could copy the pose. Inference — what the corpus shows is a two-layer attribution: the posture is credited to an Indian sage of the epics, and the poem to a named nineteenth-century Siamese gentleman. Nobody claims to have invented the cure. Everybody signs the verse.

Read this manual next to holasat and a pattern appears: the seer is what a Thai technical book puts at the point where it would otherwise have to admit it does not know where the knowledge came from.

Two seer-tricks the corpus keeps

  • ฤๅษีแปลงสาร — the Hermit's Disguise. The Vedic Katha for Chai Chatri files a short katha under this name (p.85), and the instruction is a traveller's: consecrate four leaves of the "leaf that knows how to sleep," tuck one at each side, one in front and one behind, and recite as you walk. You will not be seen — "and it protects everyone following along as well." Inference — the same sleeping leaf, and the same logic of hiding a whole party rather than one body, runs through the concealment material collected on the Khun Phaen page; the technique travels between repertoires while the name over it changes.
  • The seer who hands over his cord. In the same Compendium, a katha for awakening one's radiance (p.37) opens with a scene rather than a formula: ลินลาเทวดาทุกทิศา พระฤๅษีสิทธิ์ถอดสร้อยสังวาลย์ยื่นให้แก่กู — the devas of every quarter dance, and "the perfected lersi takes off his sacred cord and hands it to me." The next clause puts that gift straight to work in a market street where hundreds of girls turn to look. Transmission, in this register, is not a certificate. It is a man taking a thing off his own neck.
  • The seer as the source of a command. A third passage (p.58) shows the reciter drawing authority downward through a chain: ครูกูให้กูบันดาลทั้งคุณพระฤๅษี อันอยู่ในถ้ำ — "my teacher grants that I command even the virtue of the lersi who dwells in the cave." What is being consecrated at the end of all that cosmic name-dropping is face powder.

Seers who are alive, and one who is a monk

The archive holds three volumes of vipassanā kammaṭṭhāna — cutting off worry, the hindrances, ānāpānasati, the first jhāna, the ten kasiṇas, recollection of death — as orthodox a syllabus as Theravāda teaches. They are titled การทำกรรมฐาน หลวงพ่อฤาษีลิงดำ, the edition of Luang Por Ruesi Ling Dam — literally "Reverend Father Black-Monkey Hermit" — of Wat Tha Sung, Uthai Thani (vol. 1, vol. 2, vol. 3). The frontispiece is a photograph of a robed monk seated on a white cloth before a temple hall.

These are, as it happens, the only three witnesses in all 6,990 catalogue records whose title contains a lersi word at all — and the lersi in them is not a subject. He is a nickname on a modern abbot who taught jhāna. Inference — that is the cleanest possible demonstration of what the word does in living Thai: it is available as an honorific for a person of demonstrated attainment, and it carries no implication that its bearer left the Sangha for a cave.

The same continuity runs through Kammatthana for Siddhi Powers, which is a modern Thai book about mental power told partly as travel memoir. The author studies at an open-air "Natural University" in India, is caught taking medicine "as if it were food," and is told by a teacher with an inner eye: "people have something good with them already and don't know how to use it" (p.42). Later chapters describe Tibetan tummo — hermits at 11,000 to 18,000 feet in two or three pieces of clothing, or none — and set out hatha yoga as the balancing of ha, the sun, against tha, the moon (p.84, p.90). Inference — the seer here has become a comparative category: whatever a Himalayan lama, an Indian yogi and a Thai forest ascetic are all doing, this book files it under one heading and treats it as one science.

The silence in the title line, and the Lanna thread

Set beside all that, the palm-leaf catalogue's answer is stark. Across the 6,959 catalogue records outside the contributed shelf, not one title names a lersi as its subject — every spelling checked (ฤๅษี, ฤาษี, ฤษี, lersi, ruesi, reusi, rusi, rishi). The only three title matches in the whole database are the modern Wat Tha Sung volumes above.

Inference — this is a title-index absence, not an archive absence, and the contributed shelf proves it: the seer is everywhere in the contents. 25 of the 31 contributed printed volumes carry the word ฤๅษี in their transcribed text. The rite that invokes him sits on the first two leaves of books catalogued as katha-collections, yantra-manuals, ceremony handbooks and massage treatises — precisely the position a title-level index cannot see. A cataloguer writing Untitled (Katha, Nyan) on a Lanna bundle is not hiding the lersi; the lersi simply is not what the bundle is called.

There is a second thread worth naming plainly, and worth not overstating. Tradition holds — Lanna's own foundation stories credit founder-rishis with raising the northern cities: seers who established Haripuñjaya and sent for Queen Camadevi from Lavo, and the hermit whose name the mountain above Chiang Mai still carries. The catalogue does hold those chronicles — 9 witnesses whose title names Suthep, 5 naming Camadevi, 6 naming Hariphunchai, inside a tamnan corpus of 1,060. What the catalogue does not yet hold is their text: none of them has been digested to page level, so this archive cannot yet quote a single line of a Lanna seer's story from its own holdings. Inference — that is the single highest-value transcription target on this page. The seer is almost certainly inside those leaves as an actor; only a content-level pass will bring him out.

Notes

  • Read this with katha and yantra. The lersi is the authority those two forms cite. Nearly every wai khru passage above stands at the head of a katha collection or a yant manual, and the Yantra Craft of the Lanna Lineage shows the same rite in northern hands.
  • He is also the missing first page of the almanacs. The astrologer's own craft claims the same descent — see holasat and the Phrommachat, and the astrology corpus they sit in.
  • The masks are still outside the archive. Tradition holds — the hua khon lersi, the seer-mask worn or enthroned at the annual wai khru of sak-yant, dance and craft lineages, is the most publicly visible lersi object in Thailand today. No seer-mask appears anywhere in the 4,817 digested pages. The only masks the corpus mentions at all are a demon-mask nailed to a house and a boat's prow-mask, both listed among ill-omen signs in the Lanna yantra manual, and a simile comparing a stretching hermit to a khon-masked giant in the massage manual. That is a clean, shaped gap: the corpus holds the words of the wai khru in four printings and none of its objects. A photographic and vernacular-commerce pass is what would close it.
  • A crawl and transcription priority, now narrowed. The old want-list entry said "find the lersi." The corpus has answered that. What is still missing is (1) the Lanna chronicle text where he acts rather than receives homage, (2) wai khru manuals catalogued as such, and (3) named-lersi dossiers — Ta Wua, Ta Fai, Narot, Nalai and the rest are, so far, nine names in a list nobody in this archive has yet told a story about.
  • Handle with the tradition's own care. This is teacher-lineage material, and parts of it are transmitted under restriction. The Teaching of Meditation and Mind Power sets a five-year bar before a student may teach at all, and adds that anyone under it must first go through the teacher-raising ceremony (p.24); the Life of Luang Por Thob prints "the teachers have laid down a prohibition: this knowledge must not be intentionally passed on… trust this and never violate it" directly beneath the formula (p.102). Provenance travels with this material; where the tradition asks for gating, the archive should gate.
