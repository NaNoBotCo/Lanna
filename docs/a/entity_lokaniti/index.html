Lokanīti — worldly wisdom, under two names

What this is

Lokanīti (ลอกะนิติ, "guidance for the world") is a collection of maxims in Pali verse about how to live among other people — how to tell a friend from a flatterer, how to serve a king without being destroyed by him, why learning outlasts money, what a fool does with good advice. It is not a religious text in any strict sense. It is worldly, hard-headed, and frequently unsentimental, and it was copied for centuries by the same monasteries that copied the canon.

Search this catalogue for it by name and you get two manuscripts. Search for what the north actually called it and you get thirteen more, a century and a half older, filed under four different genres. That gap is the subject of this page.

The genre it belongs to

Tradition holds — Lokanīti belongs to the Pali nīti literature, the Theravada branch of an older Indian tradition of nīti — statecraft and practical wisdom — whose Sanskrit ancestors travel under names like Cāṇakya. The maxims are arranged by subject: the wise man, the fool, friends, kings, women, wealth. They are the kind of thing quoted to settle an argument, and in the Burmese and Shan world they were a schoolroom text — a boy learning Pali learned it partly by working through verses that told him not to trust a river, a clawed animal, or a man who agrees with everything he says.

They pair naturally with the archive's other law-adjacent subjects: the Thammasat, which supplies law with its cosmological warrant, and the Mangraisat, which supplies Lanna with its actual statutes. Inference — between them these three cover the whole range of "how to behave": nīti advises, dhammasattha justifies, and the Mangrai code punishes.

Two under the name

Exactly two witnesses spell it ลอกะนิติ, and both come from Mae Hong Son:

  • Wat Luang, Mae Hong Son — 145 pages, dated 1880, Shan script.
  • Ban Pang Mu, Mae Hong Son — 70 pages, dated 1889.

Inference — two copies, one province, one spelling, nine years apart, in the far northwest hard against the Burmese border — historically Tai Yai (Shan) country rather than Lanna heartland — reads as a single stream of transmission arriving from the west, where Lokanīti was standard curriculum. The poetry shelf shows the same door in use: the collection's only Khun Sam Lo is likewise a Shan manuscript from Wat Luang.

What would be wrong is to stop there and conclude the north did not keep this literature. It kept a great deal of it, under another name.

Thirteen under another

Search instead for โลกนัย / โลกไนย / โลกนัยยะ — Lokanai, Lokanainya — and thirteen witnesses appear, in Tham Lanna and Tham Lao, none of them in Mae Hong Son:

  • The oldest is ms 4417, บาลีโลกนัยย ("Pali Lokanainya"), 64 pages at Wat Lai Hin Luang in Lampang, dated 1726 — 154 years older than the oldest copy under the Lokanīti spelling, and older than anything the didactic genre holds under its own label.
  • Then a steady line through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: Wohan lokanainya at Wat Ban Luk (1743, and again inside a 1750 bundle), Lokanai at Wat Phra Bat Ming Mueang in Phrae (1806), Lokanainya at Wat Ban Ueam (1814), and a Lokanai at Wat Phra That Chang Kham in Nan (1860).
  • And then a set. Six of the thirteen are titled นิไสโลกนัยย — nisai, that is nissaya, the word-by-word Pali-into-vernacular glossing format described on the grammar & lexicography page — and they are numbered as bundles: mat ton, mat song, mat si, first, second, fourth. Five of those six are dated to a single year, 1836, four of them at Wat Sung Men in Phrae and one at Wat Suan Tan in Nan.

Inference — 1836 is the year Wat Sung Men dated 404 of its manuscripts, the copying campaign described on that temple's own page. A numbered, glossed, multi-bundle Lokanai sitting inside that year is not a curiosity someone copied once; it is a text being taught, broken into fascicles and fitted with a running translation so students could work through the Pali. Whatever else the north thought of this literature, it put it on the syllabus.

Tradition holds — whether โลกนัย is the same work as โลกนีติ, a sibling nīti collection, or a Lanna recension that drifted under a similar name, is a question this catalogue cannot yet answer — no witness of either family has been transcribed. The titles are close enough to invite the identification and different enough to forbid assuming it. That is the open question this page exists to record.

Filed everywhere but home

The thirteen are catalogued under four different genres: jātaka and Buddhist canonical take five and four respectively, customary law takes one, and didactic & moral — the genre this text defines — takes exactly one, ms 4431 at Wat Nantharam in Chiang Mai.

Inference — the cataloguers were not careless; they were reading the object in front of them. A nissaya bundle looks like a Pali study text, so it went to the canon. A maxim collection bound with tales went to jātaka. One went to law because worldly advice about rulers is not far from statute. The genre label records what a manuscript resembled, and a text that resembles four things gets scattered across four shelves — the same section-level invisibility documented for paṭiloma and Khun Phaen, here operating on a text that is not hidden inside bundles at all, merely named unexpectedly.

Browse the didactic genre and you will meet one of these thirteen. The other twelve are elsewhere.

Notes

  • Nothing here has been transcribed, so this page cannot say which verses any of the fifteen manuscripts carry, or whether the Mae Hong Son Lokanīti and the Lanna Lokanai are one work. Every description of the maxims' content above is marked Tradition holds — for that reason.
  • The single highest-value job this page can name: transcribe a few leaves of ms 4417 (Wat Lai Hin Luang, 1726) and a few of the 1880 Wat Luang ลอกะนิติ, and compare. Two openings would probably settle a question that no amount of title-searching can.
  • A caution for anyone recounting this entity. The site's own witness count for this subject follows the Lokanīti spelling only. The Lokanai family is real, older, and larger, and any statement about "how much of this the collection holds" has to say which name it is counting.
