What this is

Jātaka (Lanna chadok, ชาดก) are birth-stories: tales of the Buddha's previous lives as the Bodhisatta, working his way toward the perfections across hundreds of existences. At 1,633 witnesses this is the second-largest genre in the collection, behind only the canon itself — and unlike the canon, it is made of nothing but stories.

That is worth stopping on, because the definition contains a mechanism. A jātaka is "a thing that happened to the Buddha before he was the Buddha." Any story at all can be one, provided somebody is willing to say the hero was the Bodhisatta. The genre is therefore not a fixed list; it is an open socket — a device for taking a tale that belongs to a village, a hill, a river or a household and rendering it, without changing a single incident, into scripture. Inference — the titles held here are the best evidence of that device running at scale. Filed under the same label as the Pali nipāta groupings, in the same Tham Lanna hand, on the same palm-leaf, sit a toad who makes war on the sky, a beast with four ears and five eyes, an elephant with seven heads and seven tails, a woman who ate a hundred and one crabs, and a whole cycle of orphan boys and their animals. Nothing in the canon contains them. The catalogue calls them birth-tales anyway, and that is the finding.

The orphan and his animal

The clearest single case is a tale-type, not a tale. ก่ำพร้า (kampha / kamphra) is northern Thai for "orphan," and fifteen witnesses in this genre carry it in the title, always yoked to a creature:

  • ก่ำพร้าหมาดำ — the orphan and the black dog. Four witnesses, from Wat Thung Man and Wat Ban Caem in Lamphun (1924), Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang (1866), and one undated modern copy.
  • ก่ำพร้างัวทอง — the orphan and the golden ox (Wat Phaya Phu, Nan, 1941).
  • ก่ำพร้าหมากเต้า — the orphan and the gourd (Wat Pa O Don Chai, Chiang Rai, 1957).
  • กำพ้าฅวายโพง — the orphan and the great buffalo, twice from Wat Khuean Mueang Pa Kha Tai in Chiang Rai (1954, 1963).
  • กำพ้าหมาเน่า — the orphan and the rotten dog (Wat Phra Luang, Phrae, 1899) — a title that does not soften itself for scripture.
  • ทุคคะตะกำพ้ากุมมาน — "the destitute orphan boy," bound in one bundle at Wat Pa Sao, Lamphun (1932) together with กำพ้ากุมมานแมงสี่หูห้าตา, the orphan boy and the four-eared five-eyed beast.

Tradition holds — the northern orphan-hero cycle is one of the most widely told story-shapes in Tai-speaking Southeast Asia: a parentless boy at the bottom of the village, an animal that is not what it appears, a reversal that ends with the boy married, rich, or a king. It is a poor child's story, told to poor children. In this catalogue it has been copied onto palm-leaf in the monastery hand and shelved with the nipāta.

And the seam is visible in the titles. Three bundles from Wat Pa O Don Chai in Chiang Rai, copied between 1967 and 1971, pair an orphan tale with a text whose title begins อลอง — along, from the Burmese-Shan alaung, "one destined for Buddhahood," the being-in-progress. อลองยิงดาว is bound with กำพร้าควายโพง; อลองต๋องยวก with ก่ำพร้าหมากเต้า; a third, อลองโขงธาตุ, stands alone. Inference — that prefix is the socket itself, made visible: it is the word a scribe writes to say this one, too, was him.

The four-eared, five-eyed beast

แมงสี่หูห้าตา — "the four-eared five-eyed creature" — has four witnesses of its own: Wat Dong Mada in Chiang Rai (1934), the Wat Pa Sao bundle above (1932), a modern copy of 1973, and a substantial 126-page copy at Wat Pa Pao, Chiang Mai, dated 2001 — sixty-seven years after the Chiang Rai leaf, which tells you the tale had not stopped being wanted.

Tradition holds — the northern tellings give it as a beast found in the forest by a poor boy, an animal that eats charcoal and voids gold, and whose keeper is made rich by what comes out of it; in Chiang Rai the story is attached to local stupa-founding lore. It is a fortune-tale with a grotesque at the middle of it. Nothing remotely like it appears in the Pali five hundred. It is catalogued here as a birth of the Buddha.

The toad who fought the sky

พระยาฅางฅาก, Phaya Khankhak, the Toad King, is held in ten witnesses across the catalogue — Wat Lai Hin Luang and Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang (1852, 1853), Wat Si Khom Kham (1863), Wat Klang in Phrae (1896), Wat Dong Mada in Chiang Rai (1948), Wat Pa Sao in Lamphun (1951), Wat Hia in Nan, a bundle at Wat Ban Caem in Lamphun that pairs it with Kanthakha, one large modern copy of 1973 running to 184 pages — and, at Wat Sung Men in Phrae, a copy whose own title calls it not a birth-tale but a ตำนาน — Tamnan Phanya Khrang Khrak, the chronicle of the Toad King. All ten sit on this shelf; one of them, in its own words, thinks it belongs on the chronicle shelf instead.

Tradition holds — the Toad King is the great rain-myth of the Tai world. In the common tellings the toad-prince, slighted by the sky-lord who withholds the rain, raises an army of every creature that can be raised — nāgas, hornets, ants, bees — marches on heaven, and wins; the rain is restored on terms, and the terms are the festival that fires rockets at the sky to remind him. It is agricultural, seasonal, older than any of these copies, and about weather rather than salvation. Two hundred years of northern scribes filed it as a former life of the Buddha, and one of them filed it as history. Inference — the toad is the strongest case in the collection that this genre absorbs rather than excludes: the story is not adjusted to fit Buddhism, it is simply relabelled, and the relabelling is what preserves it.

The bestiary the canon never had

Read down the title list and the genre reveals a menagerie that no Indian recension accounts for. Each of these is catalogued as jātaka:

  • ช้างเจ็ดหัวเจ็ดหาง — the elephant with seven heads and seven tails; five witnesses, twice from Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang (1909, 1916), twice under the fuller title Wala Kumman, the elephant with seven heads and seven tails (Wat San Rim Ping, Lamphun, 1905; Wat Nakhot Luang, Lampang, 1934).
  • ช้างสามงาปลาสามเงี่ยง — the three-tusked elephant and the three-spined fish; three witnesses, one of them at Wat Sung Men.
  • สิงห์ฅายนาง — the lion and the maiden; four witnesses, only one of them dated (Wat Pa Sang Ngam, Lamphun, 1944).
  • งูอมนาง — the snake that swallows the maiden; two witnesses.
  • นางกินปู — "the woman who eats crabs," four witnesses, plus นางกินปูร้อยเอ็ด, "the woman who ate a hundred and one crabs," at Wat Hia in Nan.
  • เสือตายเสือนอน — "dead tiger, sleeping tiger"; หมูหิน, the stone pig; งัวน้อย, the little ox; แลนสองลิ้น, the two-tongued monitor lizard (this last filed under poetry, on two leaves).
  • หงผาฅำ — the swan of the golden cliff, nine witnesses across the genre, one of which (Wat Sung Men, 1890) has been dressed in a Pali prefix as Suwanna Hong Pha Kham, and another of which has escaped into verse as a ฅ่าว — the sung-poem form — pairing it with a heroine named Ammala.

Inference — the golden swan is the whole mechanism in one title-family. A scribe at Wat Sung Men gave it a Sanskritic first name, Suwanna, and it reads as canon. Another set it to the sung khao metre and paired it with a heroine, and a cataloguer duly filed that copy under poetry while its twin stayed here. The tale did not change. Only the register did — and the register is what the shelf sorts by.

Not all of it is beasts. The single most-copied titles in the genre after Mahosot (14) are พละสังขยา Palasangkhanya (9, plus 4 more as wohan glosses) and อุสสาบารส Utsabalot (9) — the latter a full-dress romance, held at Wat Hia and Wat Phumin in Nan, Wat Klang and Wat Mueang Mo in Phrae, Wat Ban Ueam in Lampang, Wat Luang Ratchasanthan in Phayao (1856, the earliest dated copy) and Wat Phra That Si Chom Thong in Chiang Mai (1921), with one undated modern copy running to 593 pages. คัทธนะ Katthana has eight witnesses; มหาวงส์แตงอ่อน, "Mahāwong the tender melon," five; จำปาสี่ต้น, the Four Champa Trees, three — one of which, at 1,046 pages, is the longest single manuscript in the entire genre. Tradition holds — Usa and Barot, and the Four Champa Trees, are among the most widely sung romances of the Tai-Lao world: a girl raised in a tower by a hermit, a prince who finds her and a king who sets a contest; four murdered royal sons who come back as flowering trees. They are love stories with body counts. They are catalogued here as lives of the Buddha.

For the plots themselves, the catalogue is silent. These are palm-leaf records with title-level metadata only, so what happens in นางกินปู is not currently knowable from this archive. That is a legitimate finding and a precise crawl target: four witnesses, ten to ninety-four pages each, all in Tham Lanna, and no transcription anywhere in the open record. See the notes below.

The fifty births, in nine bundles

Above the loose local tales sits an organised body: the paññāsa jātaka (ปัญญาสชาดก), the "fifty births" composed in mainland Southeast Asia rather than India, the great apocryphal collection and a northern speciality. A title search across the catalogue finds sixty witnesses in the ปัญญาส / ห้าสิบชาด family.

The most striking thing about them is that they arrive as a set. At Wat Sung Men in Phrae, in a burst of copying concentrated in the years around 1833–1842, the fifty births were written out in numbered bundles — mat ton (the first bundle), mat song, mat sam, mat si, mat ha, mat hok, mat cet, mat paet, mat kao — the ninth bundle and the first both present, most of them dated 1836, several of them duplicated, and the whole run standing today as a single library's decision to possess the entire apocryphal collection at once. Wat Mueang Mo in Phrae holds a partial second run, dated 1876–1877. Other copies scatter east into Nan — Wat Pa Mueat (1908), Wat Phaya Phu (1861) — and the Nan copying of the Mahāchat kan at Wat Na Pang runs earlier still, to 1784 and 1792.

Many of them are labelled นิไส (nisai) — the word for a Pali text carrying a running Lanna gloss, phrase by phrase. Inference — nine bundles of glossed apocrypha copied in a single decade is not devotional accumulation; it is a curriculum being stood up. Somebody at Wat Sung Men wanted the fifty births teachable.

The Ten Births, and the thirty editions of the greatest

The canonical spine is still here, and it is thick. Every one of the ทศชาติ, the last ten births, is present under its northern spelling: Mahosot (27 witnesses), Teminya (22), Canok (20), Nemilat (18), Phulitat / Bhūridatta (16), Canta Kumman (7), Nalata / Nārada (6), Suwannasayam and Suwannasam (8 between the two spellings), Withun / Vidhura (a Lampang speciality — Wat Ban Luk alone holds seven, the oldest dated 1722), and above all เวสสันตระ, the Great Birth: 91 witnesses in this genre alone carry it in the Thai title. It has its own page — Vessantara / Mahāchat — and it deserves it.

One manuscript does the whole job in a single line. At Wat Chedi Luang Worawihan in Chiang Mai, a palm-leaf bundle dated 1875 is catalogued under the title เตมิยะ, ชนก, สยาม, เนมิราด, มโหสถ, ภูริทัด, จันทกุมมาน, นารท, วิธูร, เวสสันตระ — the ten, in order, in one binding.

The Mahāchat's own witnesses show something else: eighty-two jātaka manuscripts carry a ฉบับ tag — a named recension. Fifty-six titles list the chapters (kan) by name — Thotsaphon, Himaphan, Thanakhan, Wannapawet, Chuchok, Chunlaphon, Mahaphon, Kuman, Matsi, Sakkaban, Maharat, Sakkatti, Nakhon — and then say which telling this is. The names are pure Lanna: ฉบับไผ่แจ้เรียวแดง (slender red-shoot bamboo), ฉบับเผิ้งกิ่งย้อย (bees on the drooping branch), ฉบับหิ่งแก้วมโนเนื่อง, ฉบับช่อฟ้ายองปลี, ฉบับแสนยอด, ฉบับร้อยกอง, ฉบับแท่นแก้ว, ฉบับอุโมงฅำ, ฉบับส้อยพ้าว, and — the one that gives the game away — ฉบับเถ้าลืมตื่น, "the edition the old man forgot to wake up from."

Geography rides in those names too: ฉบับพันนาเชียงรุ่ง (the Chiang Rung recension, from Sipsong Panna), ฉบับโสฬสสิบหกเมือง ("of the sixteen muang"), ฉบับสามเมือง, ฉบับเชียงราย, ฉบับชัยยาเชียงใหม่, ฉบับพระสิงหลวงเมืองเชียงใหม่. Inference — nobody names an edition unless listeners can tell editions apart. Eighty-two tagged witnesses is a record of a performance culture in which the audience knew whose Mahāchat they were hearing — which puts this genre as close to liturgy and to the sung khao as it is to scripture.

The extreme compression of the same text is also held: คาถาพัน, "the thousand verses" — the bare Pali gāthā of the Vessantara stripped of narrative, seven witnesses, the oldest at Wat Mueang Mo in Phrae (1816). The Great Birth exists in this collection at both ends of the dial: thirteen named chapters with a village recension attached, and a thousand verses with nothing attached at all.

The stories the printed shelf still tells

The palm-leaf catalogue gives titles. For the tales told out loud, the collection's contributed printed treatises (which carry full transcription and translation) are where the narrative survives — and what they show is birth-stories functioning as the origin-story attached to a protective chant.

The clearest run is in the Secrets of Saiyasat, which walks the paritta one by one and gives each its jātaka. The Khandha Paritta (p.39–41) opens with a monk kindling fire in the fire-hut, bitten on the toe by a snake out of a rotten tree, dead where he sat; the Buddha's verdict is that the monk had failed to cultivate loving-kindness toward the four royal families of nāgas — and then he tells the past life, in which the Bodhisatta, an ascetic at a bend of the Ganges below the Himavanta, gathers his students and teaches them to send mettā first to the Virūpakkha, Erāpatha, Chabyāputta and Kaṇhā-Gotamaka nāga kings, then outward to the footless, the two-footed, the four-footed and the many-footed. The snakes stop coming. The katha that people still chant is the ending of that story.

The Chaddanta run (p.44–48) is the best-told thing in the corpus. A nun listening to a sermon at Jetavana suddenly remembers a former life — she laughs, because she was once the elephant-king's attendant; then she weeps, because she was also the one who had him killed. The tale: the six-tusked elephant-king Chaddanta shakes a sal tree; pollen falls on the favoured consort Mahāsubhaddā, red tree-ants fall on Culasubhaddā. That is the first grudge. A lotus given to the other is the second. Culasubhaddā offers fruit to a Paccekabuddha and spends the merit on a wish — to be reborn a queen with the power to send a hunter after those tusks — then refuses food and dies into it. Reborn as queen of Vārāṇasī, she feigns a pregnancy craving, assembles the kingdom's hunters, and names Sonuttara. He walks seven years, seven months and seven days, digs a pit under the elephant's standing-place, roofs it, leaves a slot for the bow, and puts on a saffron monastic robe before he climbs down. The arrow goes in at the navel and out at the back. The elephant reaches for him to kill him, sees the robe, and stops: the robe is the banner of the arahants, and the wise do not destroy it. He lifts the hunter out of the pit with his trunk, tells him a man full of defilement should not wear it, asks who sent him, and — on hearing the answer — has his own tusks sawn off and handed over. The verses that close it (p.48) are the paritta: even pierced by the arrow he did not let his mind turn against the saffron cloth; if this is true, may no beast of the forest come at me. The palm-leaf shelf holds the same tale under its northern names — ช้างฉทัน twice at Wat Sung Men (1912, 1951), โสทันตกี and its wohan glosses there and at Wat Phra Singh in Chiang Mai (1815), and ช้างสทันตทังเก้า, "the nine Chaddanta elephants," at Wat Pa Phlu in Lamphun (1894).

The golden peacock (p.49–53) is the long one. The Bodhisatta as a golden peacock recites his own protective verse at dawn and dusk and cannot be caught. A queen wants to hear him preach and dies unsatisfied; a king, enraged, has it engraved on a gold plate that whoever eats the peacock's flesh will not age and will not die. Six kings and six hunters die trying. Six hundred and ninety-three years pass. The seventh hunter brings a peahen — and the peacock, distracted, forgets the verse and walks into the snare. Then the hunter looks at what he has caught, decides that a creature this virtuous should not suffer, and draws his bow to cut the tether; the peacock, misreading it, begs for his life. They talk. The peacock preaches abstention from killing, and the hunter attains Paccekabuddhahood standing there. The Supreme Maha Mantra Katha gives the same tale in one sober paragraph as item 16 of a chanting list (p.104): the Mora Paritta, why it works, and how the hen undid seven years of discipline.

Item 23 in the same list (p.105) is the Vattaka Paritta, "the quail chant," and it is two sentences long and complete: a forest fire, parents who fly, a chick who cannot. I have wings but cannot fly, I have feet but cannot walk; my parents have fled — let the flames retreat. The flames retreat. The Secrets of Saiyasat tells it at length (p.57), adding the frame: the fire had come within sixteen karisa of the Buddha and gone out by itself, and the monks called it a marvel of his present power, and he corrected them — it was the truth-act of the chick.

Birth-tales as working equipment

Once a story is a paritta, it is also a material. The corpus shows the birth-tales entering the magic and ritual shelf as usable parts:

  • The Ten Births as ten heart-syllables. Two independent treatises carry the same mnemonic table, in which each of the last ten births is compressed to a four-syllable key: sarattana is the Vessantara, namasang-i the Vidhura, pasi-u-a the Mahosadha, karateja the Temiya, masania the Bhūridatta, awasatha the Suvaṇṇasāma, paphayaha the Mahājanaka — filed among the hearts of Sakka, Phra Phum and the Boiling Ganges (Vedic Katha for Chai Chatri p.59; Yantra Inscription Formulas p.30). The whole moral weight of a birth-story, reduced to a key you can strike.
  • A birth-tale as a drawn na. The Scripture of 108 Magical Na (p.103) gives นะสุวรรณสาม, the Suvaṇṇasāma na, in a gallery beside the na of Sāriputta and Moggallāna — the Bodhisatta of the deer-hide and the arrow standing in the glyph roster next to the Buddha's chief disciples.
  • A parrot-birth on an oiled leaf. The Maha Yant Manual (p.101) draws a crested bird and gives the Suvapotaka formula — the young-parrot jātaka — to be laid on a rak leaf soaked in fragrant oil and consecrated 108 times, for mettā. Its sibling in Making Sacred Charms and Amulets (p.134) is the Salika-Mahosot takrut: two facing myna birds, male and female, each with its own katha, the female's invoking suvapotako … mahosuthako — the parrot and the sage Mahosadha in one line — rolled into a takrut "suited to preachers and singers." The kai page documents the same reflex with another bird: the tradition reaches for a creature's story when it wants the creature's virtue.
  • And the darkest use. In the same charm treatise (p.90), the instructions for wrapping a luk krok — a dried foetus-spirit — call for a cloth dyed with safflower, two yantras, and, to close the back yantra, the truth-verse of the baby quail: I have wings that cannot fly, feet that cannot walk, my mother and father have gone out. The verse that saved a chick from a forest fire is here laid over a dead child. Four pages earlier (p.52) a wax-salve recipe calls on Kaṇhā and Jāli, the Vessantara's given-away children, as an attraction charm, and on Jūjaka, the brahmin who begged them, as the formula for getting what you ask for. Inference — the Great Birth's villain has been reissued as a persuasion tool. Nothing in this archive shows the absorption running in both directions more plainly: the folk tale goes up into the canon, and the canon comes back down into the wax.

The Compendium of Ancient Katha (p.17) closes the circle. Its great assembly-invocation, "to be recited before undertaking anything," works through the heavens and the world-mountains and then bows to the Ten Births by name — Temiya; Mahājanaka who ordained as an ascetic; Suvaṇṇasāma who cared for his royal parents; Nemirāja the renowned; Mahosot who received the white parasol; Bhūridatta who kept the precepts; Chandakumāra; Nārada; Vidhura; the demon Puṇṇaka; Vessantara and Queen Maddī; the children Kaṇhā and Jāli. The birth-tales are being addressed the way one addresses a deity.

The shape of the collection

Physically this genre behaves like scripture: 1,529 of 1,633 in Tham Lanna (94%), 1,435 on palm-leaf (88%), with a small but real minority hand — 41 Tham Lao, 39 Shan, 18 Thai, 5 Tham Lue, 1 Burmese. Paper barely registers: 48 mulberry, 19 khoi. A jātaka is a durable sermon-text, copied into the same format as the canon it sits beside.

It is also the deepest-dated genre the collection has. Of 1,233 dated witnesses the oldest is 1471 — a Tiṁsa nipāta at Wat Lai Hin Luang in Lampang, which also holds a Tiṁsa nibat, a Paṇṇāsa nipāta and a second Tiṁsa all dated 1514, a Satta nipāta of 1526, an Eka nipāta jātaka of 1547 and an Aṭṭha nipāta of 1549. One Lampang library is effectively the whole 15th–16th-century stratum of this archive. The curve then runs 19 witnesses in the 1600s, 201 in the 1700s, 555 in the 1800s, 430 in the 1900s and three in the 2000s — a genre still being copied when the cataloguers arrived.

On provenance the collection reads as a Phrae–Lampang tradition (Phrae 443, or 27%; Lampang 357, 22%; then Chiang Mai 154, Lamphun 149, Nan 116, Chiang Rai 106, Mae Hong Son 55, Phayao 37). That shape is deceptive — see the first note.

Notes

  • The place-distribution is a library artifact, not a map of production. A single repository, Wat Sung Men in Phrae, supplies 354 witnesses, more than a fifth of the entire genre from one temple; Wat Ban Luk (122) and Wat Lai Hin Luang (104) in Lampang follow. Phrae leads because Wat Sung Men's library survived and was catalogued, not because Phrae "wrote more jātaka." Read every province and temple count in this genre — and on the atlas — against that fact.
  • Canon and apocrypha are collapsed under one label. The nipāta groupings of the canonical five hundred, the sixty-odd paññāsa witnesses, the Mahāchat's eighty-two named recensions, and the whole local bestiary all arrive as plain "Jataka" — including 82 records reclaimed from a mislabelled "Buddhist Tale" and two from "Other." Splitting these apart — canonical nipāta vs paññāsa vs Vessantara vs local tale — is the obvious next taxonomy pass, and the one most useful to a reader working from browse.
  • The local tales are the transcription priority, and they are the most exposed. For the canonical ten, a reader can go elsewhere. For นางกินปู, สิงห์ฅายนาง, ช้างเจ็ดหัวเจ็ดหาง, ก่ำพร้าหมาเน่า and the rest, this catalogue's title is very close to the whole of the open-access record — the plots exist in the leaves and almost nowhere else. Copies at Wat Pa Sak Noi, Wat San Rim Ping and Wat Khuean Mueang Pa Kha Tai run to only six or ten pages apiece: short, cheap to transcribe, and unrecoverable if lost.
  • Overlap with recitation and with verse. Because the Mahāchat is performed, expect jātaka to shade into liturgy and chanting wherever a title names a thet or a festival recitation, and into poetry wherever it names a ฅ่าว — the golden-swan tale appears in both.
  • Overlap with the teaching shelf. Vidhura the wise minister runs a career on both sides of the wall: sixteen witnesses as jātaka, and three more — Withula chai son luk, Cao Withun son lan, Khlong Withun chai son lok (Wat Pratu Pa, Lamphun, 1964) — filed under didactic and moral, where the story has been boiled off and only the advice kept. The traffic runs the other way too: seven witnesses of the Pali lokanai family — the north's maxim literature, followed on the Lokanīti page — have been filed into this genre, because a Pali title on a palm leaf reads as scripture to a cataloguer and a birth-tale is scripture's nearest shelf.
