What this is

This is the tamnan proper — the foundation-legend at its most local. Not the lineage of the Buddhas and not the succession of kings, but the story of this hill, this cave, this reclining image, this pool: why the ground here is different from the ground a kilometre away. At 328 witnesses it sits between the larger Buddhist Chronicle (569) and the smaller Secular History (159) inside the chronicle genre.

It is also, and this has to be said before any of its numbers are used, one collection. All 328 come from a single crawled source — the EFEO Lanna manuscripts project — and that source contributes nothing else to this genre. The other two sub-genres are entirely DLNTM. The three-way split is therefore, in large part, two cataloguing houses that never agreed on a vocabulary. The proof of it is below, and it is unusually clean.

What it actually holds — the very small sacred

Read the titles and the sub-genre's real character shows up at once: this is the chronicle of things you could walk to in an afternoon.

  • Ten tamnan of a พระนอน, a reclining Buddha — including ตำนานพระนอนขอนม่วง (ms 6588, ms 6785), ตำนานพระนอนหนองผึ้ง (ms 6868, Wat Photharam, Phayao, 1935; ms 6919, Wat Walukaram, 1895), and ms 6701 at Wat Chiang Man, 1936. Each large recumbent image in the north apparently generated its own paperwork, the way each relic did.
  • Seven tamnan of caves — ตำนานถ้ำเชียงดาว, the Chiang Dao cave (ms 6760, Wat Hia, Nan, 1939; and again inside ms 6885, Chiang Rai, 1949, bundled with the tamnan of Indra); ตำนานถ้ำตับเตา (ms 6821, ms 6921); ตำนานถ้ำปุ่ม, the "pitted cave" (ms 6876, Wat Kasa, Chiang Rai, 1926). Tradition holds — Tham Chiang Dao is the north's classic threshold cave: a passage said to run for days into the mountain toward a hidden city of the perfected, guarded and not to be entered casually. See Wat Tham Chiang Dao.
  • Ten tamnan of พระยาอินทร์, Indra — ms 6743 (Wat Intharam, 1922), ms 6709 Tamnan Phraya Intha Chao Fa at Wat Chiang Man (1937), ms 6738 pairing Indra with the constellation Dao Khwan (Wat Ban Hong Luang, Lamphun, 1942). In Lanna narrative Indra is the god who notices: his throne heats when merit is at stake, and he comes down to intervene. A muang with an Indra-tamnan is a muang claiming it was noticed.
  • Four tamnan of the ruesi — ตำนานพระรสี ๗ ตน, the Seven Seers (ms 6711, Wat Pratu Pa, Lamphun), ตำนานรสีทั้ง 7 (ms 6734, Wat Ban Hong Luang, 1932), ตำนานรสีโทน, the solitary seer (ms 6815, 1960), and plain ตำนานรสี (ms 6909, Wat Walukaram, 1957). These are the only ruesi-titled chronicles in the entire archive, and they are all in this batch. Anyone researching the lersi should start here: the hermit who founds a city is a Lanna commonplace, and this is where the north wrote his own story down rather than borrowing him as scenery.
  • Two tamnan of Upagutta — ตำนานอุปคุตเถร (ms 6879, Wat Mae Kham Nam Lat, Chiang Rai, 1960) and ms 6878, which files him beside the Eight Arahants (1959). Tradition holds — Upagutta is the arahant who dwells in a pavilion under the sea, in the nāga realm, and is summoned up to hold Māra off a great festival. He is not a decorative figure: the Akhom of Phra Narai teaches an invisibility mantra (p.169) as literally "the mantra the novice monk used when he went down to see the elder Upagutta" — hold breath, recite once, dive, and the water will not enter nose or mouth. The chronicle here and the working spell there are the same tradition on two shelves.
  • Four tamnan of the ท้าวทั้งสี่, the Four Lords — ms 6898 and ms 6834 (both 1921), ms 6822 (1946), ms 6625 (1958), the last two titled "the Four Lords come together in assembly." Inference — this is the sub-genre's most useful cross- reference. Across the whole archive 15 manuscripts name the Four Lords, and 11 of them are flagged research-priority — because those eleven are not chronicles but rites: ปูชาท้าวทังสี่, the offering made when a house-post goes in, when rain is asked of Phaya Thaen, when a khwan is called back, or alongside a suep cata life-extension (ms 5691, Wat Saen Mueang Ma, Phayao). Same four beings; one shelf tells their story, the other shelf puts food in front of them. That is exactly the seam this archive exists to make legible. One object elsewhere in the chronicle genre closes it entirely: ms 4361, filed as Miscellany, is a paper school notebook copied 16–18 January 1973 whose sixteen sections run ปูชาแม่ธรณี, เรียกขวัญ, ท้าวทัง ๔, จักฝังเสาเรือน in sequence. The Four Lords are section nine, about ten lines long, sitting between the calendar-omens and the timber-omens — exactly where you would want them if you were about to raise a house.

Alongside the miniatures the batch keeps the big ones too: Phra Chao Liap Lok (ms 6799, Wat Mae Wa Luang, 1922, plus ms 6794 filing the Ping-river tamnan as its ninth fascicle), Mulasasana (ms 6637, Wat Phra Luang Sung Men, 1834), Tamnan Yonaka Chiang Saen (ms 6596), three fat volumes of Tamnan Phraya Chueang (ms 6671–6673, Wat Klang, Phrae, 1895), Camadevi twice (ms 6710, ms 6880), Doi Tung three times, Suthep four times, and the Hariphunchai relic at Wat Ratanaram in 1871 (ms 6679). One title even records the library's own founder: ms 6926, ตำนานธรรมพระมหาเถรกัญจนะ — the tamnan of the great elder Kanchana — kept at Wat Sung Men, the temple whose collection dominates this whole archive.

The cataloguing signature, and what it proves

Three signals mark this batch as a discrete corpus folded into the catalogue, and unlike the usual "probably" they can be checked:

  • Language. 327 of 328 carry the identical tag Pali-Northern Thai — a label used almost nowhere else, where the archive's norm is Pali and Lan Na (653 in this genre alone). The 328th is tagged Other.
  • Romanization. The transliterated titles use a scholarly IAST-style system — tāṃnān braḥ dhātu càm dàṅ, tāṃnān yonaka jīeṅ sèn, tāṃnān braḥ rasī 7 tan — quite unlike the phonetic Tanakhan / Wetsantala style used across the rest of the collection.
  • Identifiers. Every record is numbered `001_001`, `007_003` — bundle and item — and resolves to a Drupal node URL, where the DLNTM records resolve to IIIF manifests.

And then the clincher. EFEO and DLNTM photographed some of the same temples: 20 of this batch's 44 temples also appear in the DLNTM records. At Wat Chiang Man the same bundles are demonstrably catalogued twice —

  • ms 787 Tamnan maha pha that cao laphun (DLNTM) = ms 6708 Tamnan Maha That Laphun (EFEO), both dated 1833
  • ms 782 Tamnan maha that cao chae haeng = ms 6703 Tamnan Chae Haeng, both 1815
  • ms 790 Tamnan pha that com yong = ms 6705 Tamnan Maha That Chao Chom Yong, both 1877
  • ms 785 Tamnan pha non = ms 6701 Tamnan Phra Non, both 1936
  • ms 795 Tamnan solasa, Tamnan mai maha pothi cao = ms 6702 Tamnan Mai Maha Pho lae Sorassa Sip Hok Mueang, both 1870

Five clean pairs in one nine-item bundle. Inference — this is not a flaw so much as a gift: it is a rare chance to see two professional cataloguing traditions describe the identical palm-leaf and disagree about its title, its language tag and its genre. It also means the three sub-genre counts must not be added and treated as a survival sample. They are two projects' holdings with a measurable overlap.

A second convention worth knowing: EFEO splits multi-fascicle works into separate records by phuk. Tamnan Phraya Chueang is three records (phuk 1–4, 5–8, 9–11); the Thupawong at Wat Ratanaram is three (phuk 1–3, 4–6, 8–10). Counting records here is not counting works.

The shape of the collection

Almost perfectly uniform: 327 of 328 palm-leaf, 326 of them Tham Lanna (one Tham Lao, one mulberry-paper outlier), 231 dated, running 1760–1981 — the earliest being ms 6930, ตำนานป่าไม้หิมพานต์, the tamnan of the Himavanta forest, copied at Wat Sung Men in 1760.

Geographically it leans hard to Chiang Mai (152), then Phrae (53), Lampang (33), Lamphun (24), Bangkok (21), Phayao (17), Chiang Rai (15), Nan (13), across 42 named repositories. The lead holders are Wat Pa Sak Noi (32), Wat Walukaram (29), Wat Phra Luang Sung Men (25), Wat Pa Tueng Klang Thung (17) and Wat Mae Wa Luang (17) — and, distinctively, the Siam Society (18), in Bangkok, which is a learned-society library rather than a temple. That Bangkok share is the tell of a scholarly gathering project, not a survival pattern.

Notes

  • Never quote this sub-genre's counts as a regional signal. They are one source's holdings, with 20 temples shared with the other source and at least five demonstrated duplicate records. Comparisons within the batch are sound; comparisons across the genre boundary are not, until de-duplication is done.
  • De-duplication is a concrete, finishable job. Inference — provenance temple plus date plus a normalized title gets most of the way there, and the Wat Chiang Man bundle gives a labelled test set of five known pairs to validate against. Do that and every count on the parent genre page gets sharper.
  • The ruesi and Upagutta titles are the highest-value transcription targets here. They are the archive's only chronicles of those figures, and both have live counterparts in the wichaa shelf — which means a transcription would immediately connect to something people still do. Four ruesi titles, two Upagutta titles, six manuscripts.
  • Its content overlaps its sibling by design. The relic-tamnan here are the same kind of text as the Buddhist Chronicle's relic-histories; the boundary between them is a labelling boundary, not a content one. Merging or re-sorting the two remains a live question — and the most locally-anchored material, the caves and pools and reclining images, should be walked against the wats and the atlas rather than browsed as a list.
